\title{Calibration of extrapolated logical error rates: nominal 95\% intervals of constant-prefactor fits showed low empirical coverage}

\author{Adam Faust}
\affiliation{MOBLUEHQ, Inc., Long Grove, Illinois, USA}
\date{\ifdraftmark DRAFT v14, not for distribution. \fi 25 November 2026.}

\begin{abstract}
Resource estimates for fault-tolerant quantum computers rest on logical error rates $\pL$ far too small to simulate directly; they are obtained by fitting a formula at high physical error rate $p$ and extrapolating. We ask how often their 95\% intervals contain the right value. For a rotated surface-code memory at $d=3,5,7$ under circuit-level depolarising noise with matching decoding, we obtained reference estimates at $p=10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$ independently of any fit, by importance sampling stratified by the number of faults, and scored \V{reps} replicate fits on each of two fit windows. The standard fit, with the exponent fixed at $(d+1)/2$ and a constant prefactor, is close but overconfident. Its median predictions are within \V{ratio.fixed.lowpct.max}\% of the reference on the first window ($p=4\times10^{-3}$ to $1.5\times10^{-2}$) and \V{bw.fixed.maxdev}\% on the second ($1.5\times10^{-3}$ to $4\times10^{-3}$), but its upper 95\% limits are only \V{hw.R3.min}--\V{bw.hw.R2.max}\% above the prediction. Coverage at the reference point estimates is below 90\% in \V{v6.fx.w1.pt} of \V{bw4.cells} cells on the first window and \V{v6.fx.w2.pt} of \V{bw4.cells} on the second, and stays below 90\% over the whole stated uncertainty of the reference in \V{v6.fx.w1.robust} and \V{v6.fx.w2.robust} of them. A free-exponent power law was high by up to \V{ratio.R1.d7.t6}-fold on the first window and covered \V{bw.R1.covmin}--\V{bw.R1.covmax}\% on the second, with very wide intervals. A smooth prefactor with the same leading power covered \V{am.R4p.lowd.covmin}--\V{am.R4p.lowd.covmax}\% at $d=3$ and $5$ on the first window (post hoc) and missed its registered bar on the second. No remedy is established. The reference standard errors leave several $d=7$ verdicts undecided, and neither a bias common to the references nor a failure caused by fewer than $(d+1)/2$ faults is excluded. One code, noise model and decoder; no published estimate was examined. The plan was committed privately before any coverage was computed, but after the first-window data and first reference runs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A logical qubit is useful when its error rate $\pL$ is far below the physical error rate $p$ of its parts. For the surface code \cite{dennis2002,fowler2012} the rates that matter for applications, $10^{-10}$ and below, cannot be reached by direct Monte Carlo simulation, which needs of order $1/\pL$ shots. The usual practice is to simulate where failures are frequent, fit a formula, and extrapolate. The formula is often a power law in $p$ with exponent $(d+1)/2$ for odd code distance $d$ \cite{fowler2012,litinski2019}, sometimes with a free exponent, sometimes with a correction factor \cite{bravyi2024}, and resource estimates such as Ref.~\cite{gidneyekera2021} build on the result.

\emph{The question.} When such a fit reports a 95\% interval for $\pL$ at a much lower $p$, how often does that interval contain the right value? This can be answered only where $\pL$ is known independently of the fit. We obtained independent reference estimates for the real circuit at $d=3,5,7$, and scored \V{reps} replicate fits against them on each of two fit windows.

\emph{The finding.} The standard fit, with the exponent fixed at $(d+1)/2$ and a constant prefactor, predicts well and reports error bars that are far too small. Its median predictions were within \V{ratio.fixed.lowpct.max}\% of the reference on one window and \V{bw.fixed.maxdev}\% on the other, but its upper 95\% limits were only \V{hw.R3.min}--\V{bw.hw.R2.max}\% above the prediction. The intervals contained the reference point estimate in fewer than 90\% of replicates in all \V{v6.fx.w1.pt} combinations of fit, distance and target on the first window, and in \V{v6.fx.w2.pt} of \V{bw4.cells} on the second. A reference is itself an estimate, so these are hit rates against estimates, not the coverage of the true rate; letting each reference move over its stated $\pm1.96$ standard errors leaves \V{v6.fx.w1.robust} and \V{v6.fx.w2.robust} of these cells below 90\%, but that range omits any bias shared by the references and assumes that sets of fewer than $(d+1)/2$ faults never cause a failure. A likely reason is that the interval carries only the sampling error of the simulated points; it has no term for the slow change of the prefactor between the window and the target. Freeing the exponent is not a cure: that fit was high by up to \V{ratio.R1.d7.t6}-fold on one window, and on the other it covered the reference in \V{bw.R1.covmin}--\V{bw.R1.covmax}\% of replicates, with closer point predictions but intervals whose upper limit is \V{v7.sc.ul} times the lower at $p=10^{-6}$. We found no fit that we can recommend.

\emph{What is new.} We measure how often the nominal 95\% interval of a conventional fit in $p$ contains an independently estimated logical error rate at lower $p$: the repeated-sampling coverage of such intervals. Three lines of earlier work come closest. (i) Methods that reach low error rates without a fit in $p$: the splitting method of Bravyi and Vargo \cite{bravyivargo2013,mayer2025}, the sampling of fault subsets of fixed size of Heussen \emph{et al.}\ \cite{heussen2024,ye2026}, and related estimators \cite{beverland2025,molavi2026}. These estimate $\pL$ itself, and they are what makes our question answerable: our reference estimator is our own implementation of the fault-number approach. They do not say how far the fits that are still widely used can be trusted, and in the parts of these works we read, none reports how often a fit's interval contains an independently estimated rate. (ii) Experimental papers extrapolate in code distance $d$ at fixed $p$ by assuming a constant error-suppression factor $\Lambda$ between successive distances \cite{google2023,google2025}, also without a measured interval coverage. Here we test extrapolation in $p$; our main distance projection, predicting $d=7$ from fits at $d=3$ and $5$, failed mainly because those underlying extrapolations in $p$ missed, and the same projection to $d=9$ is compared only with an interval that rests on an unproven assumption (\SM\ Table~\ref{S-tab:cert}), so we do not test whether $\Lambda$ is constant. (iii) That fits can extrapolate badly is known from examples: Appendix~A.3 of Ref.~\cite{beverland2025} reports it for one form on a code of even distance, and Ref.~\cite{mullan2026} shows that a decoder which mishandles one hook error can make a convincing high-$p$ fit underestimate the failure rate at low $p$. Those are wrong point predictions, not measured frequencies. No systematic survey has been made, and we do not claim priority.

The plan, its bars and the code were committed to a private repository before any coverage was computed, but after the first-window data and the first reference runs had been generated and the checks of the reference estimator seen; the study is therefore registered only in that qualified sense. Several registered predictions failed, and analyses made afterwards are called post hoc (\SM\ Table~\ref{S-tab:status} and Sec.~\ref{S-sec:moved}). The archive is at \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24543057} (release v1.1).

\section{Design}\label{sec:design}
\subsection{Circuit, decoder and units}\label{sec:units}
The circuit is the rotated surface-code memory experiment in the $Z$ basis generated by Stim \cite{stim2021}, with $d$ rounds at distance $d$. The noise is uniform circuit-level depolarising: a depolarising channel of strength $p$ after every Clifford gate, and a flip with probability $p$ before each measurement and after each reset. Decoding is minimum-weight perfect matching with PyMatching \cite{sparseblossom2025}, with edge weights taken from the circuit's detector error model at the same $p$.

$\pL$ is always the \emph{block} rate, the probability of a logical error per memory experiment of $d$ rounds. Rates at different distances are compared \emph{per round},
\begin{equation}
\epsilon(d)=\tfrac12\bigl[1-(1-2\pL)^{1/d}\bigr]\approx\pL/d ,\label{eq:perround}
\end{equation}
as is conventional \cite{google2023,google2025}, and $\Lambda=\epsilon(d)/\epsilon(d+2)$.

\subsection{The fits}\label{sec:recipes}
A fit window has five values of $p$, each simulated in chunks of fixed size until the first chunk at which at least 300 logical errors have been seen (so the shot count is not fixed in advance and $\hat\pL=k/n$ is used as it stands), with \V{reps} independent replicate datasets at each of $d=3,5,7$. The \emph{first window} is $p\in\{4,6,8,10,15\}\times10^{-3}$. Inside it the curves for different distances cross (Sec.~\ref{sec:fig1}). The \emph{second window}, $p\in\{1.5,2,2.5,3,4\}\times10^{-3}$, lies below that crossing. It was added after the first-window results were known, and its position was set by a rule that uses neither the new data nor any reference estimate (\SM\ Sec.~\ref{S-sec:moved}). The two windows differ in three ways at once: the second lies below the crossing, it is narrower (its ends differ by a factor of \V{bw.span.new}, against \V{bw.span.old}), and it is \V{bw.gapdiff} decades nearer the targets. The design cannot assign a difference between the windows to any one of these.

Table~\ref{tab:recipes} lists the fits. R2 and R3 fix the exponent at $(d+1)/2$ with a constant prefactor, R2 for each distance separately and R3 with one curve for all three. R1 frees the exponent. R4$'$ keeps the leading power and lets the prefactor vary smoothly. R5 is not fitted separately: it takes the R1 fits at $d=3$ and $5$ and applies the suppression between them once more, to project $d=7$, as registered on block rates (Sec.~\ref{sec:r5}). R1 to R4$'$ are fitted to each replicate by weighted least squares on $\ln\hat\pL$, where $\hat\pL=k/n$ for $k$ failures in $n$ shots and the weight of a point is $k/(1-\hat\pL)$. Each fit reports the 95\% interval it would naturally report: $\hat\mu\pm1.96\,\hat\sigma$ for $\ln\pL$ at the target, that is $[e^{\hat\mu-1.96\hat\sigma},e^{\hat\mu+1.96\hat\sigma}]$ for $\pL$, from the binomial errors as they stand. The \emph{half-width} quoted below is the upper end relative to the prediction, $e^{1.96\hat\sigma}-1$. These are our implementations of common forms, not a reconstruction of the procedure of any particular paper.

\subsection{Scoring}\label{sec:metric}
The targets are $p=10^{-3},10^{-4},10^{-5},10^{-6}$. A replicate \emph{hits} if the reference estimate of $\pL$ lies in its interval, and \emph{coverage} is the fraction of the \V{reps} replicates that hit. A \emph{cell} is one recipe at one distance and one target. We call a cell miscalibrated if its coverage is below 90\% and calibrated if it lies in 93--97\%; these are screening rules, the cells are not independent (the four targets of a row reuse each replicate's fit), and exact binomial intervals \cite{clopperpearson1934} for every cell are in \SM\ Table~\ref{S-tab:covrange}.

Because the reference is itself an estimate, every verdict is also given over a \emph{reference range}: the smallest and largest hit fraction as the reference value moves over its estimate $\pm1.96$ standard errors. A verdict of below 90\% is \emph{robust} if the whole range is below 90\%, and a cell whose range straddles 90\% is \emph{undecided}. The range is post hoc.

\subsection{Reference estimates}\label{sec:ref}
The values against which the fits are scored come from importance sampling stratified by the number of faults, an established approach \cite{heussen2024,beverland2025,ye2026,molavi2026}; our implementation is specified in \SM\ Sec.~\ref{S-sec:method}. Stim's detector error model of the circuit at the target $p$ is a list of $N$ independent fault mechanisms ($N=\V{m.N.d3}$, \V{m.N.d5} and \V{m.N.d7} at $d=3,5,7$). With $W$ the number of mechanisms that occur,
\begin{equation}
\pL=\sum_{w\ge1}P(W=w)\,f_w ,\label{eq:strata}
\end{equation}
where $f_w$ is the probability of failure given exactly $w$ faults. $P(W=w)$ is computed exactly, and each $f_w$, for $w=1$ to $120$, is estimated by drawing fault sets of that size and decoding them with the decoder of Sec.~\ref{sec:units}. Strata receive draws adaptively until a working standard error is at most 2\% of the estimate or a limit of time or draws is reached. Because allocation and stopping depend on the draws already made, the estimate is not exactly unbiased and its standard error is not a guaranteed bound. Strata with $w<(d+1)/2$, in which none of $10^5$ draws failed in any run, are set to zero, which for the circuit is an assumption. Each of the twelve reference values ($d\in\{3,5,7\}$ by four targets) combines two independent runs.

Figure~\ref{fig:money} shows the first window with the leading power divided out. Write $\pL=p^{h}A(p)$ with $h=(d+1)/2$. At the low-$p$ targets the reference prefactor $A$ is nearly constant for $d=3$ and $5$ and dips by about 15\% at $d=7$ at the two middle targets. Inside the window it is still falling at all three distances, so the local slope of $\ln\pL$ against $\ln p$ is less than $h$. A constant prefactor (R2, R3) averages over the fall and lands below the plateau. A free exponent (R1) reads the fall as a shallower slope and extrapolates high. A smooth prefactor with the right leading power (R4$'$) can follow the fall and return to the plateau. In every case the interval propagates only the binomial sampling errors of the window points; none has a term for the drift of the prefactor.

\subsection{How far the reference can be trusted}\label{sec:truthcheck}
The verdicts are only as good as the reference, so we checked it in three ways (\SM\ Secs.~\ref{S-sec:method}, \ref{S-sec:refs}). Against direct Monte Carlo on the real circuit the estimator agreed within $|z|\le2$ in \V{val.ok} of \V{val.eligible} cells, but direct validation stops at $\pL\approx\V{val.pmin}$, while the reference estimates extend to $\V{truth.d7.t6}$. Below the validated range they rest on the estimator's own error analysis and on checks against a linearised surrogate of the error model, whose polynomial through fourth order in $p$ was computed exactly at $d=3$ and $5$ and is a truncation. The check against the surrogate had a limited outcome. The working estimator's intervals were checked only against the truncated fourth-order polynomial, which they contained in \V{v6.br.is.touch} of \V{v6.br.is.n} runs (four cells of 20 seeds). The full-bracket check of \SM\ Table~\ref{S-tab:bracket} applies to a different estimator, the intervals with a stated guarantee of \SM\ Sec.~\ref{S-sec:cert}, and decides only at $d=3$, where each of them contains the whole bracket (\SM\ Sec.~\ref{S-sec:shells}). The two independent runs behind each reference value agree (largest $|z|=\V{truth.zmax}$). The combined relative standard error is \V{truthse.lowd.min}--\V{truthse.lowd.max}\% at $d=3$ and $5$, but \V{truthse.d7.far.min}--\V{truthse.d7.far.max}\% at $d=7$ for the three lower targets, where all runs stopped at their time limit. A downward bias of about 10\% in the $d=7$ references at the middle targets, from heavy fault sets missed by both runs, is neither shown nor excluded.

At $d=7$ this uncertainty is larger than the intervals being scored. The R2 intervals there are $\pm$\V{hw.R2.max}\% wide, and the first-window hit fraction of R2 at $p=10^{-4}$ is \V{v6.r2d7.w1.min.t4}\% against one reference run and \V{v6.r2d7.w1.max.t4}\% against the other (\SM\ Table~\ref{S-tab:r2d7}). Several $d=7$ verdicts are therefore undecided, and we draw no conclusion from those cells.

On the second window the fits are closer, and the conclusion is the same but less sharp. The median predictions span \V{bw.ratio.fixed.min}--\V{bw.ratio.fixed.max} times the reference. The upper limits are again close to the prediction, \V{bw.hw.R2.max}\% (R2) and \V{bw.hw.R3.min}--\V{bw.hw.R3.max}\% (R3) above it, so a bias of at most \V{bw.fixed.maxdev}\% is enough to defeat them. Coverage is below 90\% in \V{v6.fx.w2.pt} of 24 cells, more than the 16 our registered prediction required. Here the bias is comparable with the uncertainty of the reference, so fewer verdicts are robust: \V{v7.w2.R2.all.below} of 12 for R2, with \V{v7.w2.R2.all.undecided} undecided, and \V{v7.w2.R3.all.below} of 12 for R3, with \V{v7.w2.R3.all.undecided} undecided. R3 carries a further bias because its single curve is forced through three distances: at $d=5$ it predicts \V{bw.ratio.R3.d5.t4}--\V{bw.ratio.R3.d5.t3} of the reference, against \V{bw.ratio.R2.d5.t4}--\V{bw.ratio.R2.d5.t3} for R2. At the better-resolved distances $d=3$ and $5$ on this window, coverage stays below 90\% throughout the reference range in \V{v7.w2.R2.lowd.below} of \V{v7.w2.R2.lowd.n} cells of R2 and \V{v7.w2.R3.lowd.below} of \V{v7.w2.R3.lowd.n} of R3 (\SM\ Table~\ref{S-tab:covrange}), the rest undecided. In neither window does any cell of R2 or R3 stay at or above 90\% over the whole range.

Simple repairs do not help. Scaling the covariance by $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ changes little (\V{bw4.sca.mis} of \V{bw4.cells} cells below 90\% on the second window; \SM\ Table~\ref{S-tab:bwsca}), and two registered attempts to widen the intervals, by a discrepancy variance estimated from a held-out window point and by dividing out the zero-fault factor, failed their bars (\SM\ Sec.~\ref{S-sec:inflate}).

\subsection{The free-exponent fit depends on the window}\label{sec:bw}
On the first window R1 over-predicts, and the factor grows with each decade: at $d=7$ the median prediction is \V{ratio.R1.d7.t3}, \V{ratio.R1.d7.t4}, \V{ratio.R1.d7.t5} and \V{ratio.R1.d7.t6} times the reference at $p=10^{-3}$ to $10^{-6}$. Its 95\% half-widths are \V{hw.R1.min}--\V{hw.R1.max}\% of the prediction, wide but not wide enough for errors of that size. Coverage is at most \V{cov.pri.max}\%, robustly in all \V{v6.R1.w1.robust} cells.

We predicted that this severe undercoverage would persist on the second window, and it did not. R1's hit fraction there is between \V{bw.R1.covmin}\% and \V{bw.R1.covmax}\% in all twelve cells (median \V{bw.R1.median}\%; \SM\ Fig.~\ref{S-fig:win}), and its median prediction is \V{bw.ratio.R1.min}--\V{bw.ratio.R1.max} times the reference. Over the reference range \V{v6.R1.w2.above} of the 12 cells stay at or above 90\% and \V{v6.R1.w2.strad} are undecided. The price is the width of the interval: the median 95\% half-width is \V{bw.hw.R1.t3.max}\% of the prediction at $p=10^{-3}$, \V{bw.hw.R1.t4.max}\% at $10^{-4}$, \V{bw.hw.R1.t5.max}\% at $10^{-5}$ and \V{bw.hw.R1.t6.max}\% at $10^{-6}$, where the upper limit is \V{v7.sc.ul} times the lower. The coverage is also a little short of nominal: \V{bw3.inband.R1} of the 12 cells are in the 93--97\% band and none reaches 95\%.

The fitted exponents show what happened. Their medians are \V{slope.d3}, \V{slope.d5} and \V{slope.d7} on the first window and \V{bw.slope.d3}, \V{bw.slope.d5} and \V{bw.slope.d7} on the second, against \V{nominal.d3}, \V{nominal.d5} and \V{nominal.d7} for $(d+1)/2$. R1 responds to the local slope across its window, which on the first window is well short of the leading power (Sec.~\ref{sec:fig1}). We read its miss as an effect of prefactor curvature, and the crossing of the curves as a rough marker of where that curvature is large. The run establishes only that on one window, chosen by a fixed rule to lie below the crossing, the failure we had predicted for R1 did not occur.

\subsection{A smooth prefactor, and a step in distance}\label{sec:exp}\label{sec:r5}
R4$'$ is the form $p^{(d+1)/2}\exp(c_0+c_1p+c_2p^2)$. On the first window, where it is post hoc, it covered the reference in \V{am.R4p.lowd.covmin}--\V{am.R4p.lowd.covmax}\% of replicates in all eight cells at $d=3$ and $5$, with median predictions within \V{am.R4p.lowd.ratmin}--\V{am.R4p.lowd.ratmax} of the reference. On the second window it was run under a bar registered in advance: coverage in 93--97\% in at least \V{r4b.bar} of the \V{r4b.cells} cells at $d=3$ and $5$. \emph{The bar was not met}: \V{r4b.inband} of \V{r4b.cells} cells are in the band. The \V{r4b.above} misses are all at $d=3$, at $p\le10^{-4}$, where the hit fractions, \V{r4b.above.covmin}--\V{r4b.above.covmax}\%, are above the band; no cell is below 90\%. At $d=7$ most verdicts on both windows are undecided, with reference ranges reaching down to \V{v6.R4p.w1.d7.rgmin}\% and \V{v6.R4p.w2.d7.rgmin}\%. We do not call R4$'$ calibrated, and it is not a recommendation: it was chosen after the fact, and the right exponent is not sufficient, since R2 has it and fails.

R5, the projection of the R1 fits at $d=3$ and $5$ to $d=7$, failed by the largest factors in the study on the first window. As registered it applied the suppression step to block rates, an error of ours that overstates the $d=7$ rate by $\V{v6.alg}=\V{v6.algval}$ in the small-rate limit; with per-round rates it is still \V{v6.r5.w1.rat.pr.t3} to \V{v6.r5.w1.rat.pr.t6} times the reference. At $p\le10^{-4}$, \V{v6.r5.w1.share.t4}\% to \V{v6.r5.w1.share.t6}\% of the logarithm of that error comes from the R1 inputs, not from the step in distance, and a departure from constant per-round suppression is not established (\SM\ Sec.~\ref{S-sec:r4}).

\section{Conclusions and limitations}
For a rotated surface-code memory at $d=3,5,7$ under uniform circuit-level depolarising noise, extrapolated in $p$ to $10^{-3}$--$10^{-6}$, the nominal 95\% intervals of fits with the exponent fixed at $(d+1)/2$ and a constant prefactor showed low empirical coverage against independent reference estimates on both windows, although their point predictions were close. A free-exponent power law was far off on a window straddling the crossing of the curves and, with very wide intervals, covered on a window below it, where we had predicted severe undercoverage to persist. The leading power with a smooth prefactor covered about 95\% at $d=3$ and $5$ on the first window, post hoc, and missed its registered bar on the second. We suggest, without having established a remedy, that the binomial error of a fixed-form fit not be read as the error of an extrapolation; that the fitted local slope across the window be reported beside $(d+1)/2$; that the leading power be checked against the smallest number of faults that defeats the decoder actually used; and that a rate be stated as per memory experiment or per round before $\Lambda$ is formed.

These statements are only as good as the references. At the lowest rates they rest on two seeds of one estimator, whose common bias is not quantified, on the assumption that strata with $w<(d+1)/2$ faults contain no failures, and on working standard errors; direct Monte Carlo checks reached only $\pL\approx\V{val.pmin}$, and the same twelve values score both windows. The study covers one code family, noise model and decoder, memory experiments with $d\le7$, and two windows of five points whose comparison is confounded with span and with distance from the targets. The fits are our plain implementations of common forms; no published estimate was examined, and extrapolation in $d$ at fixed $p$ is touched only by R5. An interval with a stated guarantee was built for one further cell, $d=9$ at $p=10^{-4}$: it is $\V{c9.L}$ to $\V{c9.U}$ under the unproven assumption that no set of four or fewer faults causes a failure, and $\V{c9.free.L}$ to $\V{c9.free.U}$ without it (\SM\ Sec.~\ref{S-sec:cert}). The methods of Refs.~\cite{bravyivargo2013,heussen2024,beverland2025,ye2026,molavi2026,mayer2025} are not compared, in particular not at equal computing cost.
